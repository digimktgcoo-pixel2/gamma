What EPA RadNet Actually Is

Most people have never heard of RadNet until they start looking into nuclear preparedness — and that's exactly the problem this site was built to solve. EPA RadNet is the United States' primary real-time radiation air monitoring network, operated by the Environmental Protection Agency's Office of Radiation and Indoor Air. It consists of over 140 fixed monitoring stations positioned across the continental United States, Alaska, Hawaii, and U.S. territories, continuously measuring ambient gamma radiation in the air 24 hours a day, 365 days a year.

The system was designed with one core purpose: to give federal and state emergency managers early warning of abnormal radiation events — nuclear accidents, radiological releases, and in a worst-case scenario, nuclear detonations. It has been operating continuously since 1978, predating the internet entirely. The data it generates is federal public information, available to anyone who knows how to access it. NuclearThreatWatch.org makes that access effortless.

Each RadNet station is a fixed, permanently installed air monitoring unit housed in a protective enclosure. Stations are typically placed at government facilities, university campuses, and monitoring sites chosen to provide geographic distribution across populated areas. Stations near nuclear power plants are particularly valuable — they provide the earliest publicly visible signal of any unusual emission from those facilities, often before any official announcement is made.

The Two EPA Data Systems: CDX vs. Envirofacts

This is where most people — including many developers who've tried to build on EPA data before — get confused. The EPA doesn't just have one radiation database. It has two distinct systems that serve completely different purposes, and mixing them up creates serious problems for a live monitoring application.

CDX RadNet (the Environmental Dataset Gateway's RadNet REST endpoint) is what powers the live map on NuclearThreatWatch.org. It delivers near-real-time CSV data by state and city, accessed through a Cloudflare Worker proxy that handles the API calls server-side. This keeps the data pipeline fast, avoids CORS issues, and ensures your browser isn't making direct calls to federal servers on every page load.

Envirofacts is a completely separate EPA database — better described as an environmental data warehouse. It contains not just radiation readings, but water quality data, air quality data, chemical releases, and more. For radiation specifically, Envirofacts holds post-QC finalized data — meaning it's more thoroughly reviewed, but also significantly delayed. It's the right tool for building historical trend pages, downloadable data archives, and research-focused interfaces. NuclearThreatWatch.org will use it for exactly those purposes in future phases, but it would be the wrong choice for a live map where freshness is everything.

Why this split matters to you: When you check the live map and see a reading labeled "Live," that data came through the CDX endpoint within the last posting cycle. When you see "Stale," the station's last CDX posting is older than our freshness threshold. When you see "Estimated," the CDX fetch failed entirely and we're showing a baseline fallback rather than hiding the station. Every status label traces back to one data decision: never blend uncertain data in silently.

How the Live Data Pipeline Works Step by Step

Understanding the path data takes from an EPA sensor to your screen helps you interpret what you're seeing — and recognize the difference between a real spike and a data transmission artifact.

  • Step 1 — Sensor reads the air: Each RadNet station continuously measures ambient gamma radiation using a sodium iodide (NaI) scintillation detector. These detectors are calibrated to measure gamma radiation specifically — the penetrating radiation type most relevant to nuclear event monitoring.
  • Step 2 — Data transmitted to EPA: Station readings are transmitted electronically to EPA's CDX servers on an automated schedule. Transmission can be delayed by equipment issues, connectivity problems at the station, or EPA system maintenance.
  • Step 3 — EPA posts to CDX API: Once received, readings are posted to the CDX RadNet REST API endpoint organized by state and city. This posting target is roughly hourly, but actual post times vary. EPA applies basic quality control checks before posting.
  • Step 4 — Cloudflare Worker proxy: When your browser loads NuclearThreatWatch.org, our Cloudflare Worker makes the API call to EPA's CDX endpoint on your behalf. This solves cross-origin resource sharing (CORS) restrictions and adds a thin caching layer to reduce redundant federal API calls.
  • Step 5 — Status labeling: The site compares the timestamp of each returned reading against our freshness threshold. Live readings get a green label. Readings older than the threshold get a Stale label. Stations that return no data get an Estimated fallback label with a clearly displayed note that live data is unavailable.
  • Step 6 — Map and station grid render: Readings are plotted on the SVG map and displayed in the station grid below it. The same data powers both views, so the detail panel and the card grid are always consistent with each other.

What the Radiation Numbers Actually Mean

RadNet measures gamma radiation in nanoGray per hour (nGy/h) — sometimes also expressed as nanoSievert per hour (nSv/h), which is numerically equivalent for gamma radiation purposes. For non-scientists, here is a plain-language breakdown of what you're looking at:

  • Normal background radiation in the U.S.: Typically 5–100 nGy/h depending on geographic location, altitude, and local geology. Areas with granite bedrock, higher elevation, or proximity to certain industrial sites will read higher than sea-level coastal areas.
  • Elevated reading: A reading meaningfully above the established local baseline for that station — not necessarily dangerous on its own, but worth monitoring over subsequent hours to see if it persists or increases.
  • Watch level: A sustained reading significantly above baseline that would warrant attention from emergency managers. At this level, understanding the trend over time matters more than the single data point.
  • Dangerous exposure levels: According to the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, radiation doses above 100 millisievert (mSv) begin to show measurable increased cancer risk. A single RadNet reading in the nGy/h range represents a tiny fraction of that — normal background radiation over an entire year adds up to roughly 3 mSv total for most Americans. The concern with nuclear events isn't the ambient air reading alone — it's sustained exposure, inhalation of radioactive particles, and ingestion of contaminated food or water over time.

Key insight: A single elevated RadNet reading in isolation is rarely cause for immediate alarm. What matters is the trend over consecutive readings, whether multiple nearby stations show the same pattern simultaneously, and whether the spike correlates with any known event — a reactor shutdown, a weather event moving a plume, or confirmed emergency management activity in the area. This is exactly why checking NuclearThreatWatch.org regularly matters: you need to know your local baseline to recognize a genuine anomaly.

Why Station City Names Don't Always Match the Map

One of the least-documented quirks of the RadNet system is that EPA's official station city names frequently don't match the common geographic name for a location. A station physically located in a suburb of a major city may be listed under a small township name that doesn't appear on any consumer map. A station near Chicago may be listed under a municipality name that Chicagoans have never heard of.

This creates a real problem when making API calls: if you query the CDX endpoint using the common city name and EPA's internal name is different, you get no data back — and it looks like the station is offline or nonexistent. NuclearThreatWatch.org maintains a curated station mapping table that cross-references EPA's official location names against geographic coordinates and common labels, so the live map correctly resolves readings to the right locations on screen. This took significant research to build correctly and is one of the reasons the station directory is a core part of the site rather than an afterthought.

What "Stale" and "Estimated" Data Really Mean

RadNet stations occasionally go offline. Transmission gets interrupted. EPA applies QC holds to data before posting. Servers get overloaded during high-demand events (exactly the moments when you most need the data). NuclearThreatWatch.org handles all of these scenarios explicitly rather than pretending they don't happen.

  • Stale data means the most recent CDX posting for that station is older than our threshold. The reading shown is real EPA data — it's just not fresh. The timestamp is always visible so you can judge the age yourself.
  • Estimated data means the CDX endpoint returned no usable data for that station during the most recent fetch cycle. Rather than showing a blank or removing the station from the map, we display a baseline estimate and label it clearly. An estimated reading should never be used to assess current conditions — it's a placeholder that tells you the station is in our network but temporarily unreachable.

This approach — label instead of hide — is a deliberate design philosophy. A map that silently removes stations when data is unavailable creates a false impression of coverage. During a real nuclear emergency, the stations that go offline first are often the ones closest to the event. Knowing which stations are not reporting is as important as knowing which ones are.

The Envirofacts Roadmap — What's Coming Later

The current version of NuclearThreatWatch.org intentionally limits itself to the CDX live pipeline so the live map remains stable and fast. But the EPA's Envirofacts system opens up a significantly richer set of capabilities for future phases:

  • Historical trend pages — Query radiation readings for a specific city or station over days, weeks, or months. See how readings changed before and after a nuclear facility event or weather pattern. Identify long-term baseline drift.
  • State drilldown pages — Dedicated pages for each U.S. state showing all RadNet stations, their historical baselines, and proximity to active nuclear facilities.
  • Lab result data — Envirofacts includes beta radiation, precipitation, milk sampling, and other radiological lab results that go far beyond gamma air monitoring. These are relevant for tracking long-range contamination from nuclear events.
  • Downloadable archives — Export-friendly table interfaces for researchers, journalists, and emergency planners who need bulk data.

None of this is live yet. Every future feature described above would require the Envirofacts API integration to be built and tested separately from the live map pipeline so the two systems never interfere with each other. That clean separation is the entire reason the current architecture was designed the way it was.

Safety Tip

EPA RadNet tells you what the air monitoring network detects — not what a Geiger counter in your hand would read. Fixed monitoring stations are miles apart. A local radiation event near your home might not register at the nearest RadNet station if you're between stations. If you live within 30 miles of an active nuclear facility, a personal Geiger counter gives you hyperlocal readings that no government network can replicate.
