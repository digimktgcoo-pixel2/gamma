About NuclearThreatWatch.org

NuclearThreatWatch.org exists because finding reliable, real-time radiation data in a readable format has historically required digging into government databases most people have never heard of. This platform pulls live gamma radiation readings from the EPA RadNet sensor network — the same federal infrastructure emergency managers rely on — and puts them on a map anyone can read in under ten seconds. It stays free through advertising so public access to this data never sits behind a paywall.

NuclearThreatWatch.org is a free public radiation monitoring platform that displays live gamma readings from the EPA RadNet nationwide sensor network on an interactive U.S. map. It is built and operated by DigiMktg Co. LLC, developed by Eric Glas out of Quincy, IL. The site is fully independent — not affiliated with any government agency — and is funded through advertising so it can remain free to the public indefinitely. The goal is straightforward: put federal radiation data in front of ordinary people without making them learn how to use a government API to see it.

Every reading on NuclearThreatWatch.org originates from the EPA RadNet program — a permanent, nationwide network of fixed gamma radiation air monitors operated by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. Data is retrieved through the EPA CDX API on an hourly cycle. No third-party data providers are involved. No readings are fabricated or interpolated without a clear label marking them as such.

A stale reading means the most recent usable data for that station is older than the site's freshness threshold. This does not mean something alarming is happening. EPA RadNet monitors go offline regularly for calibration, equipment maintenance, and telemetry issues — it is a routine part of network operations. Stale readings are always flagged visibly and are never silently passed off as current data. If you see a stale flag on a station you care about, check back in a few hours or consult EPA RadNet directly for station status information.

EPA air-monitor location identifiers are technical slugs assigned during network deployment. They do not always match the nearest city's common name. NuclearThreatWatch.org uses display name overrides in these cases so the data retrieval path functions reliably while the map labels remain readable to the public. The underlying EPA station data is never altered — only the human-facing display label is adjusted.

An estimated reading is a fallback value displayed only when a live EPA data row cannot be retrieved for a specific station during the current hourly cycle. Estimated values are always clearly labeled so they cannot be confused with a confirmed live-feed response. The site will never display a synthetic value as a verified current reading.

Understanding Radiation

Most people encounter the word "radiation" in alarming headlines and walk away with little practical understanding of what the numbers mean or when to act. The answers below are grounded in guidance from the CDC, the FDA, and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission. They are educational only — not a substitute for medical advice or official emergency guidance.

Background radiation is the naturally occurring ionizing radiation present everywhere on Earth at all times. It originates from four primary sources: cosmic rays streaming in from space, radon gas seeping up from soil and rock, trace radioactive minerals naturally present in building materials and the ground, and low-level radioactivity inherent in food and the human body itself. The U.S. average background dose is approximately 310 millirem (mrem) per year — roughly 0.035 microsieverts per hour at ground level. This is the baseline against which EPA RadNet readings are compared, and it varies meaningfully by geography, elevation, and local geology.

Brief exposures below 100 millisieverts (mSv) are not associated with measurable increases in cancer risk or other health effects, according to the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission. Health risk rises with dose above that level, particularly when exposure occurs in a short time window. Acute Radiation Syndrome can begin at whole-body doses above 1,000 mSv (1 Sv) received rapidly. For context: a chest X-ray delivers roughly 0.1 mSv, and a full-body CT scan is approximately 10 mSv. The EPA and NRC set public annual dose limits well below 100 mSv for routine, non-emergency situations. The readings shown on NuclearThreatWatch.org under normal conditions are many orders of magnitude below these thresholds.

Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS) — commonly called radiation poisoning — results from high-dose whole-body exposure in a short time period. It is not caused by the background-level readings EPA RadNet monitors under normal conditions. Symptoms progress through distinct stages:

  • Prodromal stage (0–48 hrs after exposure): nausea, vomiting, headache, fatigue, and fever. Onset speed and severity correlate with dose received.
  • Latent stage: symptoms subside temporarily and the person may feel relatively normal. Duration ranges from days to weeks depending on dose.
  • Manifest illness stage: bone marrow suppression, compromised immune function, potential hair loss, and internal bleeding at higher doses.
  • Recovery or death depending on total dose, time elapsed before medical treatment, and individual health factors.

ARS is a life-threatening medical emergency. If you believe you have been exposed to a high-level radiation source, call 911 immediately and follow CDC radiation emergency guidance. Do not rely on any monitoring website — including this one — as a diagnostic tool for radiation exposure.

A single elevated reading on one station does not confirm a public health emergency. RadNet sensors can produce anomalous readings due to equipment malfunctions, calibration drift, or highly localized events that carry no regional risk. Before taking any action:

  • Check whether neighboring stations in the same region show similar elevation — isolated spikes are more likely sensor issues than widespread events.
  • Monitor official sources: FEMA Ready.gov, your state emergency management agency, and the Emergency Alert System (EAS).
  • Do not evacuate based solely on anything you see on this site. Evacuation decisions require official authorization and coordinated emergency management.
  • If multiple stations in your area are elevated simultaneously and official alerts are being issued, follow DHS nuclear preparedness guidance and shelter in place until told otherwise.

Potassium iodide (KI) is a non-prescription medication that saturates the thyroid gland, blocking it from absorbing radioactive iodine (I-131) that may be released following a nuclear power plant accident or certain nuclear detonation scenarios. KI protects only the thyroid gland — it does not protect the rest of the body from radiation exposure of any kind. It is not a general-purpose radiation treatment and does not reduce whole-body dose.

According to FDA guidance on potassium iodide, KI should only be taken on the explicit direction of public health officials or emergency management — not preemptively based on anything shown on a monitoring website. The FDA-recommended dose is 130mg for adults and 65mg for children. Taking KI without a genuine radioactive iodine release event can cause serious side effects, particularly for people with thyroid conditions or iodine sensitivity.

Safety Tip

Know your shelter-in-place basics before an emergency. In a radiological event, going inside a sturdy building and closing all windows, doors, and ventilation reduces your exposure significantly. A brick or concrete structure offers substantially more protection than a wood-frame home. Keep a battery-powered or hand-crank NOAA weather radio on hand — it is the most reliable alert channel when cell networks are overloaded. Review FEMA's nuclear preparedness checklist now, not after the fact.

Quick Reference

Radiation Dose Units

Microsievert (µSv) — unit used on this map. Typical background: ~0.1 µSv/hr.

Millisievert (mSv) — 1,000 µSv. U.S. annual average background: ~3.1 mSv.

Millirem (mrem) — U.S. customary unit. 1 mSv = 100 mrem.

Readings 2–3× background sustained over time warrant monitoring. Readings 10× background or higher with concurrent official alerts: follow emergency guidance immediately.
