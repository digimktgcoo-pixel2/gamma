Why NuclearThreatWatch Exists

Nuclear risk is not a hypothetical. It is a documented, formally acknowledged, actively planned-for reality — addressed in official guidance by FEMA, the Department of Homeland Security, and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission. The United States operates 93 commercial nuclear reactors at 54 active sites. Global geopolitical tensions — between nuclear-armed nations — are at levels not seen since the Cold War. And yet, the most useful piece of federal infrastructure for civilian radiation awareness — EPA's RadNet monitoring network — is buried in government portals designed for technical researchers, not families trying to make fast decisions under pressure.

NuclearThreatWatch was built to fix that. We pull live data directly from the EPA CDX RadNet REST API, render it on an accessible map updated hourly, label every reading with its data status — Live, Stale, or Estimated — and present it in plain language. No filtering. No editorial delays. No government PR timing. When EPA publishes a reading, this site shows it.

Our Mission Statement

Our mission is to do our part to help humanity survive. That sounds large — because it is. But the mechanics are straightforward: nuclear preparedness information has historically been siloed, technical, and inaccessible to the average person. The people most likely to be harmed in a nuclear emergency are also the least likely to have access to real-time monitoring data, know how to interpret a radiation reading, or understand what immediate actions could reduce their exposure. NuclearThreatWatch exists to change that equation, one informed household at a time.

We are not alarmists. This site runs ads to cover basic operating costs — hosting, data infrastructure, and maintenance — and nothing more. If you see an advertisement on NuclearThreatWatch.org, it exists to keep the lights on, full stop. We believe the best version of this tool is one that people check weekly as a routine, build familiarity with over months, and almost never need for an actual emergency. We measure success by the number of people who know their local radiation baseline before a crisis — not by traffic spikes during one.

What the Gamma Project Is — and Why It Matters

The underlying platform — codenamed Gamma — is the technical infrastructure behind NuclearThreatWatch. Gamma handles the live data pipeline from EPA's CDX endpoint through a Cloudflare Worker proxy, resolves the city-name mapping mismatches that exist between EPA's official station names and common geographic labels, and surfaces hourly readings with source-aware status labels so stale data is never silently presented as fresh.

The Gamma architecture is built PWA-first — meaning NuclearThreatWatch functions as an installable app directly from the browser, with no app store required. This matters enormously in an actual nuclear emergency scenario: app stores become unreliable under mass demand, cell networks degrade, and time becomes the scarcest resource. A home-screen PWA with cached data loads even on congested or partially offline connections. Install NuclearThreatWatch today, before you need it.

Data Sources & Transparency

Every data point on NuclearThreatWatch is traceable to a federal source. Primary radiation readings come from the EPA RadNet Program, the national network of fixed radiation monitoring stations operated by the Environmental Protection Agency since 1978. Station metadata and geographic context is cross-referenced against the NRC's operating reactor unit list. Potassium iodide guidance follows FDA dosing recommendations. Emergency response framing references FEMA's Ready.gov nuclear guidance.

We do not invent readings. We do not suppress readings. When EPA data is delayed or a station returns no data, we label it Stale or Estimated rather than filling the gap with guesswork. Honest data handling is a core part of the product — not an afterthought.

What This Site Is Not

NuclearThreatWatch is an informational tool, not a replacement for official emergency guidance. It is not a government service. It is not a medical resource. It does not issue evacuation orders or emergency alerts. In the event of a genuine nuclear emergency, always follow the guidance of your local emergency management agency and FEMA. This site exists to give you better context before and during such events — not to replace official channels.

Long-Tail Topics We Cover for Nuclear-Aware Readers

Beyond the live map, NuclearThreatWatch is building a reference library designed to answer the specific, unfiltered questions that nuclear-aware Americans are actually searching for: real-time radiation levels near nuclear power plants in the U.S., how to read a radiation monitoring station reading, what radiation dose levels are dangerous versus normal background, nuclear fallout shelter options for suburban and rural homes, potassium iodide (KI) dosing for adults and children during a nuclear emergency, World War 3 nuclear fallout wind pattern modeling, how far from a nuclear explosion is considered safe distance, and what to do in the first 24 hours after a nuclear attack or reactor meltdown. We treat these as legitimate preparedness questions, not conspiracy theory — because that is exactly what they are.

Our Commitments to You

Transparency

Honest Data — Always

EPA station names do not always match common city labels. NuclearThreatWatch corrects for those mismatches in code and in editorial notes, so geographic approximations never silently break the live data path. Stale readings are labeled. Estimated fallback data is flagged. You always know what you're looking at and how fresh it is. We will never blend stale data in with live data without telling you.

Public Trust

Timestamps Are Part of the Reading

A radiation number without freshness context is easy to misread — and in a nuclear emergency, misreading data is dangerous. NuclearThreatWatch treats the timestamp as a first-class part of every reading, not a footnote. If a station's last update was six hours ago, that is the first thing you see. Real-time nuclear threat monitoring only works if the data's age is as visible as the data itself.

Safety Tip

Practice makes response automatic. Visit NuclearThreatWatch.org once a week and spend 90 seconds checking the radiation readings for the two or three EPA monitoring stations nearest your home. After a few weeks, recognizing your local baseline becomes effortless — which means you'll immediately recognize when something is abnormal. In a genuine nuclear emergency, that recognition is worth more than any amount of gear.
