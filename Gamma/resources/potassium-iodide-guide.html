Medical Preparedness

Potassium Iodide (KI):
When & How to Use It

KI is the most widely misunderstood item in nuclear preparedness. It protects only the thyroid — not the whole body — and only against radioactive iodine specifically. Taking it at the wrong time, in the wrong dose, or for the wrong type of event can cause real harm. This guide covers everything you need to know, sourced directly to FDA guidance.

What Potassium Iodide Actually Does

Potassium iodide (KI) is a stable, non-radioactive form of iodine. When taken in the correct dose before or shortly after exposure to radioactive iodine (I-131), it saturates the thyroid gland with stable iodine — filling the uptake sites so the gland cannot absorb the radioactive form. The thyroid preferentially concentrates iodine from the bloodstream regardless of whether that iodine is stable or radioactive, which is what makes it vulnerable in specific nuclear events and what makes KI effective when used correctly.

KI does not protect the rest of your body from radiation. It does not shield against gamma radiation, beta particles from other isotopes, or any other form of radiation exposure beyond thyroid uptake of radioactive iodine specifically. It is not a general-purpose radiation treatment and should never be thought of as one. If you are building an emergency kit and wondering whether KI covers your radiation exposure concern comprehensively, the answer is no — review our family emergency checklist for the full picture of what a complete preparedness kit requires.

When KI Is and Is Not Appropriate

KI is only appropriate in scenarios where radioactive iodine has been or is expected to be released into the air. This primarily means two situations: a nuclear power plant accident that releases radioactive material into the environment, or a nuclear weapon detonation that produces radioactive fallout containing I-131. It is not appropriate for dirty bomb scenarios involving other isotopes, radon exposure, medical radiation, or any other form of radiation concern that does not specifically involve airborne radioactive iodine.

Critically, the decision to take KI should never be made based on a monitoring map, a news report, or personal judgment about risk. According to FDA guidance on potassium iodide, KI should only be taken on the explicit direction of public health officials or emergency management authorities who have confirmed that radioactive iodine has been released and that your location falls within the affected area. Taking KI without a genuine I-131 release event can cause thyroid suppression, allergic reactions, and other adverse effects — particularly for people with thyroid conditions.

FDA-Recommended Dosing by Age Group

The FDA's official KI dosing guidance is based on age, body weight, and thyroid sensitivity. The thyroid's sensitivity to radiation-induced damage is highest in infants and young children, which is why dosing is calibrated carefully by age group. The table below reflects current FDA recommendations.

Timing Matters as Much as Dose

KI is most effective when taken before or within 1–4 hours of exposure to radioactive iodine. Effectiveness diminishes significantly if taken more than 4 hours after exposure, and taking it more than 8 hours after provides minimal thyroid protection. This timing dependency is why having KI in your emergency kit before an event matters — obtaining it after an alert has been issued may be too late for it to work effectively. It also underscores why taking it preemptively without an official directive wastes the protective window for a real event.

Who Should Not Take KI Without Medical Supervision

KI is contraindicated or requires medical oversight for several groups. If you fall into any of the following categories, consult a physician before stocking KI and discuss the plan with your doctor before any emergency occurs:

  • People with thyroid disease, including hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, or thyroid nodules
  • People with known iodine allergy or sensitivity
  • People with dermatitis herpetiformis (a skin condition associated with gluten sensitivity)
  • People with hypocomplementemic vasculitis
  • Adults over 40, where the risk-benefit calculation differs significantly from younger adults and children
  • People currently taking lithium, which can interact with thyroid function

How to Obtain KI Before You Need It

Potassium iodide tablets are available without a prescription in the United States at most pharmacies, directly from manufacturers, and through online retailers. The two FDA-approved forms most commonly available to consumers are 65 mg tablets (typically marketed for children, can be split or dissolved) and 130 mg tablets for adults. Some states distribute free KI to residents living within 10 miles of nuclear power plants — contact your state emergency management agency or check the NRC reactor location list to determine whether you live in a distribution zone.

Store KI at room temperature away from moisture and light. Check expiration dates periodically — tablets have a shelf life of several years. If you live near a nuclear facility, consider including KI in your emergency supply kit alongside your shelter-in-place supplies.

What KI Cannot Do — Critical Misconceptions

The most dangerous misconception about potassium iodide is that it is a radiation antidote. It is not. Understanding what KI cannot do is as important as knowing what it can.

  • KI does not protect against gamma radiation from fallout, nuclear detonations, or any other external source.
  • KI does not protect against radioactive cesium, strontium, plutonium, or most other fallout isotopes — only against radioactive iodine uptake by the thyroid specifically.
  • KI does not reduce whole-body radiation dose in any meaningful way. If you are in a high-dose environment, KI protects only one gland.
  • KI is not effective after the thyroid has already absorbed radioactive iodine. Once absorbed, the damage is done. KI must be taken before or very shortly after exposure to work.
  • KI does not replace shelter-in-place protocols. Getting inside and reducing your overall exposure via shelter-in-place procedures is far more comprehensive protection than KI alone.

Safety Tip

KI in your kit is insurance — not a plan. The most important steps in a nuclear emergency are getting inside a sturdy structure immediately, sealing ventilation, and awaiting official guidance. KI supplements those steps for the specific scenario of a nuclear power plant accident or detonation that releases radioactive iodine. If you live within 10 miles of a nuclear power plant, check whether your state provides free KI to nearby residents — many do, and it costs you nothing. For everyone else, KI is a low-cost addition to a complete emergency kit that is worth having without being the centerpiece of your plan.
