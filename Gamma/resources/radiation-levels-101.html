Radiation Levels 101:
Units, Doses & Medical Thresholds

Millisieverts, microsieverts, grays, rems — the unit system around radiation is confusing by design. This guide cuts through it: what each unit measures, how they relate, what the U.S. average background dose looks like, and the specific thresholds at which medical attention becomes necessary.

Why Radiation Has So Many Units — And Which One Matters Most

Radiation measurement has multiple unit systems because radiation does multiple things — it delivers energy, and it affects biological tissue in ways that vary depending on the type of radiation. Physicists, medical professionals, and emergency managers all historically developed their own measurement frameworks, and the overlap creates genuine confusion for anyone trying to understand what a reading means.

For the purpose of public radiation monitoring — what you see on the NuclearThreatWatch.org live map and on personal Geiger counters — the unit you need to understand most is the microsievert per hour (µSv/hr). Everything else can be understood in relation to it. If you are new to personal detectors, our Geiger counter buyer's guide explains how to read these units on your specific device.

The Units Explained

Gray (Gy) — Physical Energy Absorbed

The gray measures the raw physical energy deposited by radiation in tissue — one gray equals one joule of energy absorbed per kilogram of material. It does not account for the biological effectiveness of different radiation types. A gray of alpha radiation does more biological damage than a gray of gamma radiation. The gray is primarily used in medical and physics contexts. You will rarely see it on a consumer monitoring device.

Sievert (Sv) — Biological Dose Equivalent

The sievert is the gray adjusted for biological effectiveness — it accounts for the fact that some radiation types damage tissue more efficiently than others. For gamma radiation (the type primarily measured by EPA RadNet), one gray equals one sievert. For emergency preparedness purposes, the sievert and its fractions are the most important unit to understand because dose limits, health thresholds, and emergency guidelines are all expressed in sieverts.

Because the sievert is a large unit, most practical readings use fractions: millisievert (mSv) — one thousandth of a sievert — and microsievert (µSv) — one millionth of a sievert. The NuclearThreatWatch.org map displays readings in µSv/hr.

Rem and Millirem — The U.S. Traditional Units

The rem (Roentgen equivalent man) and millirem are the older U.S. customary units equivalent to the sievert system: 1 sievert = 100 rem, and 1 millisievert = 100 millirem. U.S. regulatory documents, particularly older NRC publications, often use millirem. If you see a dose limit expressed as "100 millirem per year," that is the same as 1 millisievert per year.

What U.S. Background Radiation Actually Looks Like

Background radiation is the naturally occurring ionizing radiation present everywhere on Earth. In the United States, the average annual background dose from all sources is approximately 310 millirem (3.1 mSv) per year, according to the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission. That breaks down to roughly 0.035 µSv/hr as a national average at ground level — though real readings vary substantially by location.

Sources of background radiation in the U.S. include:

  • Radon gas — the largest single source, averaging about 2 mSv/yr. Varies enormously by geology and home construction.
  • Medical imaging — X-rays, CT scans, and nuclear medicine procedures add variable dose depending on individual healthcare history.
  • Cosmic radiation — higher at altitude. Denver residents receive roughly twice the cosmic dose of Miami residents.
  • Terrestrial radiation — from naturally radioactive isotopes in soil, rock, and building materials.
  • Internal sources — naturally radioactive potassium-40 and carbon-14 in food and the human body itself.

Dose Thresholds — When Does Radiation Become Dangerous?

The table below translates dose levels into plain-language health context. These thresholds reflect guidance from the CDC and NRC and apply to acute (short-period) whole-body exposure unless otherwise noted.

DoseEquivalentHealth Context
3.1 mSv/yr310 mrem/yrU.S. average annual background. No health concern.
50 mSv/yr5,000 mrem/yrNRC annual dose limit for radiation workers. Still no measurable health effect at this level.
100 mSv10,000 mremLowest acute dose associated with a small measurable increase in cancer risk over a lifetime. Still no immediate symptoms.
500 mSv50,000 mremTemporary reduction in white blood cell count may begin. No ARS symptoms at this level.
1,000 mSv (1 Sv)100,000 mremAcute Radiation Syndrome onset threshold. Nausea and vomiting within hours. Seek medical care immediately.
2,000–6,000 mSv200,000–600,000 mremSevere ARS. Bone marrow damage, significant risk of death without medical treatment.
Above 6,000 mSvAbove 600,000 mremLethal range without immediate intensive medical intervention. Median lethal dose (LD50) is approximately 4–5 Sv whole-body.

When to Seek Medical Help After Radiation Exposure

The readings shown on NuclearThreatWatch.org under normal conditions represent ambient background levels many orders of magnitude below the thresholds in the table above. A reading of 0.10 µSv/hr on the map is not a health emergency — it is ordinary background.

Medical attention is warranted if you have reason to believe you received significant acute whole-body radiation exposure — for example, if you were in close proximity to a known radiological source, a nuclear facility accident, or a nuclear detonation event. If a detonation event has occurred, consult our shelter-in-place and 7-10 rule guide for immediate action steps. Early symptoms of Acute Radiation Syndrome that should prompt immediate emergency care include:

  • Nausea and vomiting within minutes to hours of a known or suspected exposure event
  • Skin redness, burning, or blistering without an obvious thermal cause
  • Headache, fever, and dizziness following a suspected high-dose exposure
  • Hair loss beginning within days of a suspected exposure event

If you experience any of these after a potential high-dose event, call 911, follow CDC radiation emergency guidance, and do not rely on any monitoring website — including this one — as a diagnostic tool. A reading on a public sensor map cannot tell you what your personal dose was.

How This Relates to What You See on the Map

NuclearThreatWatch.org displays ambient gamma radiation in µSv/hr pulled from the EPA RadNet network. These are environmental air readings, not personal dose measurements. A reading of 0.08 µSv/hr at a station near you means the general gamma environment in that area is at that level — it does not account for your specific location, time of exposure, or proximity to any localized source. For personal dose estimation, a calibrated personal dosimeter or Geiger counter at your actual location is required. The map gives you the regional picture. Personal monitoring gives you your specific picture.

Safety Tip

The vast majority of radiation exposure most Americans will ever receive comes from medical imaging, not environmental sources. A single chest CT scan delivers approximately 7 mSv — more than two years of average background from natural sources. Understanding this context helps calibrate realistic concern. Environmental radiation at background levels is not a meaningful health risk. It is the acute, high-dose exposures from industrial accidents or nuclear events that require response — and those are exactly what NuclearThreatWatch.org monitors for at the regional level.
