How-To Guide

Gamma Radiation Monitoring
at Home

A network map tells you regional ambient levels. A personal detector tells you what is happening at your exact location right now. This guide explains how to set up home monitoring correctly — baseline establishment, detector placement, reading interpretation, and the most common mistakes that produce false alarms.

Why Home Monitoring Complements — But Does Not Replace — Network Data

The EPA RadNet network is excellent at what it does: it provides a continuous, nationwide picture of ambient gamma radiation levels at fixed sensor locations. NuclearThreatWatch.org makes that data readable in real time. But a federal sensor station may be 30, 50, or 100 miles from your home. Regional ambient data and your specific household data are not the same thing — geology, building materials, and proximity to localized sources all create real variation at the individual address level.

A personal Geiger counter or radiation monitor at your location gives you your actual reading, not the nearest federal station's reading. The two should be broadly consistent under normal conditions. A significant, persistent divergence is worth investigating. Understanding how to use both together — regional network data for pattern recognition, personal monitoring for local verification — gives you a much more complete picture than either source alone.

Step 1: Establish Your Personal Background Baseline

The single most important thing you can do before any emergency is to know what normal looks like in your specific home. Without a baseline, any reading could seem alarming. With a baseline, you immediately know whether a reading is elevated, and by how much.

To establish your baseline correctly:

  1. Place your detector in the main living area of your home — not near the basement, not near granite countertops, not near a smoke detector, and not near any medical equipment. A stable tabletop location in an interior room is ideal.
  2. Let the detector run and log readings at consistent intervals for at least seven days — ideally two weeks. Note the reading at the same time each day and average them.
  3. Note any outliers — single spikes that appear once and do not recur are almost always cosmic ray events or detector noise, not meaningful radiation events.
  4. Record your average baseline reading in µSv/hr and keep it somewhere accessible — written down, not just in your phone.

Your personal baseline is what you compare all future readings against. It may differ from the nearest EPA RadNet station reading — that is normal and expected. What matters is consistency in your location over time.

Step 2: Understand What Affects Indoor Readings

Before you interpret a reading as elevated, understand the natural sources of variation inside a typical U.S. home. These are common causes of readings that look high but are entirely normal:

Building Materials

Granite countertops and tile can contain trace amounts of uranium, thorium, and radium — naturally radioactive minerals that release low-level gamma radiation continuously. Placing your detector directly on or near granite countertops will produce a higher reading than placing it in the center of a carpeted room. This is not dangerous — it is geology. Measure your detector away from these surfaces for your baseline.

Radon Gas

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas that seeps up from uranium-bearing soil and rock. Basements and lower floors in high-radon regions show significantly higher readings than upper floors. The EPA maintains radon zone maps by county. If your home is in a high-radon area, expect your basement readings to be noticeably higher than your upper floor readings — and keep your primary monitoring detector on an upper floor for ambient comparison purposes.

Altitude and Cosmic Radiation

At higher elevations, the atmosphere provides less shielding from cosmic radiation. Residents of Denver, Albuquerque, Salt Lake City, and other high-altitude cities naturally experience higher background readings than residents of coastal cities at sea level. If you travel frequently between elevation extremes and bring your detector, the readings will reflect geography, not a radiation event.

Smoke Detectors

Ionization-type smoke detectors contain a small amount of americium-241, a radioactive element that ionizes air to detect smoke. Placing your Geiger counter directly next to a smoke detector will produce an elevated reading. Keep your monitoring detector at least several feet away from any ionization smoke detector.

Step 3: Interpreting Elevated Readings Without Overreacting

Once you have your baseline, any reading above it warrants a brief, methodical evaluation — not an immediate emergency response. Work through these steps in order:

  1. Is the reading persistent? A single elevated reading that returns to baseline on the next measurement is almost certainly a cosmic ray event or detector noise. These happen regularly. Document it and continue watching.
  2. Is it localized to one spot? Move the detector to a different room. If the elevated reading disappears, you have a localized source — likely a building material or other normal radioactive object. If the reading is consistent throughout the house, it is an ambient event worth investigating further.
  3. What does the regional map show? Check NuclearThreatWatch.org to see whether nearby EPA stations show any elevation. A personal reading that diverges from all surrounding regional stations is almost always a local source, not a widespread event.
  4. Is it 2–3× your baseline or more? A reading 2–3× your normal that persists across multiple measurements and is confirmed by regional station elevation warrants monitoring official sources. A reading 10× your baseline with corresponding regional elevation and official alerts warrants action per FEMA guidance — review our shelter-in-place and 7-10 rule guide so you know exactly what to do.

Using Home Monitoring Alongside NuclearThreatWatch.org

The right workflow is to use both sources together. NuclearThreatWatch.org gives you the regional EPA picture — dozens of federal sensor stations across your state and neighboring states, all in one view. Your personal detector gives you your specific location. Under normal conditions they should broadly agree. The useful signal is when they diverge persistently — either your home reads significantly higher than nearby EPA stations for reasons you cannot attribute to local sources, or regional stations are showing elevation that your personal monitor confirms locally. If you are unsure what your readings mean in terms of health thresholds, our Radiation Levels 101 guide covers every unit and dose threshold in plain language.

Neither source makes decisions for you. The EPA RadNet program, FEMA, and your local emergency management agency issue the guidance that drives action. Monitoring tools — network-based and personal — provide situational awareness that helps you stay informed while you wait for that official guidance.

Safety Tip

Log your baseline readings in a simple notebook, not just your phone. In a power outage or cellular network failure scenario, a written record of your normal readings is accessible when a phone may not be. Include the date, time, room location, and µSv/hr reading for each entry. Two weeks of baseline data written down is one of the most useful preparedness steps you can take with a personal radiation monitor.

Baseline Guide

Normal Reading Ranges

0.05–0.10 µSv/hr — coastal, low altitude, typical indoor background

0.10–0.30 µSv/hr — higher altitude, granite geology, older building materials — still normal

0.30–1.00 µSv/hr — above typical, confirm with nearby EPA stations

Above 1.00 µSv/hr — persistent elevation warrants monitoring official sources
