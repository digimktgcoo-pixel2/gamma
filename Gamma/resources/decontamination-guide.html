Emergency Response

Nuclear Fallout
Decontamination Guide

Radioactive contamination on the surface of your body or clothing is largely removable — but only if you know the correct procedure and perform it promptly. This guide covers decontamination for people, children, pets, and your home, sourced to CDC and FEMA protocols and written to be usable under stress.

Why Decontamination Works — and Why Speed Matters

Nuclear fallout consists of radioactive particles — fragments of fission products and vaporized material that have condensed and settled from the atmosphere. When these particles deposit on exposed skin, hair, or clothing, they become a source of ongoing radiation exposure that moves with you. Unlike the ambient gamma field in the outside environment — which you reduce by going indoors — surface contamination continues irradiating you from contact until it is physically removed.

The good news is that surface decontamination is highly effective. According to FEMA Ready.gov guidance, removing outer clothing alone eliminates approximately 80% of external contamination. Adding a thorough shower with soap and water removes the vast majority of the remainder. You do not need specialized equipment, chemical agents, or professional assistance to perform effective personal decontamination. What you need is knowledge of the correct procedure and the supplies to execute it — both of which you can have ready before any emergency occurs. Review our family emergency checklist to make sure your kit is complete.

Speed matters because the longer particles remain on your skin, the greater the dose you absorb from them — and the longer they have to potentially transfer from surface contamination into internal contamination through hand-to-mouth contact or inhalation of disturbed particles. Decontaminating promptly after potential exposure significantly reduces total dose. This principle connects directly to understanding the difference between exposure and contamination — surface contamination is removable; absorbed dose is not.

01

Remove Outer Clothing — Do This First

Before entering your home or shelter room, remove your outer clothing outside or in a garage, mudroom, or other transitional space — not inside your primary shelter area. Outer clothing including coats, shoes, and outer layers captures the majority of surface fallout particles. Removing it before entering prevents tracking contamination into your clean shelter space.

  • Remove clothes by rolling them inward — fold contaminated outer surfaces toward the inside as you remove items to minimize disturbing particles into the air.
  • Place in a plastic bag and seal it — double-bag if possible. Label it and keep it away from people and pets.
  • Remove shoes and leave them outside or in the transition space — shoe soles pick up surface contamination directly from the ground.
  • Do not shake clothing — shaking resuspends particles into the air where they can be inhaled.
02

Shower Correctly — Technique Matters

Shower as soon as possible after removing outer clothing. The goal is to wash particles off the surface of your skin and hair without driving them into your pores or causing skin breaks that could facilitate internal contamination.

  • Use lukewarm water and soap — hot water opens pores; cold water may cause discomfort and reduce thoroughness. Gentle soap is fine.
  • Shampoo your hair with shampoo — do not use conditioner. Conditioner is designed to bind to hair and can trap particles rather than rinse them away.
  • Gently scrub skin — moderate pressure is correct. Do not scrub hard enough to abrade skin or open any cuts.
  • Pay special attention to hair, scalp, hands, fingernails, and skin folds — these areas trap particles more effectively than flat skin surfaces.
  • Blow your nose gently and wipe the inside of your nostrils with a clean damp cloth after showering.
  • Wipe your eyelids and lashes with a clean damp cloth — from the inside corner outward.
  • Clean your ears with a damp cloth — do not insert anything into the ear canal.
  • Put on clean, dry clothes from inside your home — clothing stored indoors will not have been exposed to outdoor fallout.
03

Decontaminating Children

Children require the same decontamination steps as adults but need extra care in execution. Children's skin is more sensitive, and young children cannot self-decontaminate. Have a designated adult perform the procedure while minimizing the child's distress.

  • Remove children's outer clothing first the same way as adults — roll inward, bag and seal.
  • Shower or bathe children immediately using the same lukewarm water and soap protocol. Keep water out of eyes and ears as much as possible.
  • Wash hair gently — no conditioner. Rinse thoroughly.
  • For infants and toddlers who cannot stand for a shower, a gentle bath with clean water and mild soap is acceptable. Change bath water once to ensure thorough rinsing.
  • Dress in clean indoor clothing after decontamination.
04

Decontaminating Pets

Pets that were outdoors during a fallout event should be decontaminated before being allowed into your shelter space. Their fur can carry surface particles and re-expose household members who pet or handle them.

  • Keep pets out of your shelter room until decontaminated — confine them to a bathroom, mudroom, or other non-shelter area first.
  • Wear disposable gloves when bathing pets if available.
  • Bathe with pet shampoo and lukewarm water — rinse thoroughly. No conditioner.
  • Wipe paws thoroughly — paws contact ground surfaces directly.
  • Dry with a clean indoor towel and allow into shelter space once dry.

Decontaminating Your Home

If your home was breached during a fallout event — windows left open, HVAC running, or occupants tracking contamination in before decontamination was possible — surface decontamination of the home may reduce ongoing dose rate inside. This is a secondary concern after personal decontamination, but worth addressing once all occupants are clean and sheltered.

  • Remove and bag any outdoor items brought inside before the event — shoes, bags, umbrellas. Seal in plastic and move to a non-shelter room.
  • Wipe hard floors with damp mops or cloths — work from the interior of the room toward the door to avoid re-contaminating clean areas.
  • Vacuum carpets if possible — a HEPA-filter vacuum is preferred. Bag and seal the vacuum contents immediately after.
  • Wipe hard surfaces — countertops, doorknobs, furniture — with damp cloths. Rinse cloths frequently or use disposable wipes.
  • Wear N95 mask and gloves while cleaning if available — decontamination disturbs particles temporarily.

A personal Geiger counter is helpful for verifying that decontamination has been effective — a reading taken near the floor or recently cleaned surfaces should trend toward your established indoor baseline. See our home radiation monitoring guide for baseline establishment techniques.

Safety Tip

Have your decontamination supplies staged before you need them. A plastic bin near your entry point containing extra garbage bags, disposable gloves, and a change of clothes for each household member lets you execute the clothing-removal step without going through your shelter space first. Stage this now, while nothing is happening. In an actual event, the seconds spent searching for a garbage bag while contaminated clothing is still on are seconds of unnecessary dose accumulation.

Quick Reference

Decon Priority Order

1. Remove and bag outer clothing — eliminates ~80% of contamination.

2. Shower with soap and shampoo — no conditioner.

3. Wipe face, nose, eyes, ears with damp cloth.

4. Dress in clean indoor clothing.

5. Decontaminate children and pets.

6. Then address home surfaces.
