Nuclear Fallout:
The 7-10 Rule

The 7-10 rule is one of the most important and least-known concepts in radiological emergency planning. This guide explains what it means practically — how long to shelter, when it becomes safer to move, and why the first 24 hours after a nuclear detonation are the most critical window for reducing your total dose.

What Is the 7-10 Rule?

The 7-10 rule is a simplified framework for understanding how rapidly nuclear fallout radiation intensity decreases over time. It states: for every 7-fold increase in time after a nuclear detonation, the radiation intensity decreases by a factor of 10.

In plain terms: if the radiation level at 1 hour after detonation is 1,000 units, then at 7 hours it will be approximately 100 units. At 49 hours (7 × 7), it will be approximately 10 units. At 2 weeks, levels have typically dropped to a fraction of their initial intensity. This is not a linear decay — it is a rapid exponential drop driven by the short half-lives of the most dangerous fission products in fresh fallout.

The 7-10 rule is a planning approximation, not a precise calculation — actual decay rates depend on the specific weapon, yield, altitude of detonation, and local conditions. But it provides a critical practical insight: time is your most powerful tool against fallout exposure. The longer you can stay sheltered during the initial hours and days, the lower your total dose will be. Why the First 24 Hours Are Critical

The most dangerous period following a nuclear detonation is the first 24 hours. During this window, the shortest-lived and most intensely radioactive fission products — iodine-131, barium-140, and others with half-lives measured in hours to days — are at their peak activity. Dose received in this window can be many times higher than the dose received in all subsequent days combined, even if you remain outdoors for weeks afterward.

This is the physics behind the core shelter-in-place recommendation: getting inside and staying inside for the first 24 hours alone dramatically reduces your total radiation dose compared to being outside. You do not need a specialized fallout shelter to benefit meaningfully from this — any sturdy building, particularly one with concrete or brick walls and a basement, provides significant shielding during this critical window.

What Provides the Best Protection Inside a Building?

Not all indoor locations are equal. Radiation shielding effectiveness depends on the amount of mass between you and the fallout particles settling on exterior surfaces and the ground. Listed from most to least protective:

  1. Underground basement with concrete walls — best readily available shelter in most residential buildings. Concrete and soil provide excellent gamma shielding.
  2. Interior rooms on middle floors of multistory concrete or brick buildings — distance from the roof (where fallout settles) and from exterior walls reduces dose significantly.
  3. Interior rooms on middle floors of wood-frame multistory buildings — less shielding than masonry, but significantly better than exterior walls or ground floor near windows.
  4. Interior rooms on ground floor of single-story wood-frame homes — least effective of the above options, but still meaningfully better than being outdoors.

Being in the center of any building, away from windows, exterior walls, roof, and ground-floor exterior surfaces, reduces your exposure. The goal is mass between you and the outside world.

When Is It Safe to Leave Shelter?

The decision to leave shelter should not be made based on a monitoring website, a personal Geiger counter reading, or any other individual judgment in a radiological emergency. It should be made based on official guidance from your local emergency management agency, state health department, or FEMA.

That said, the physics provides useful context for understanding official timelines when they are issued. By the two-week mark, total fallout intensity has typically dropped to a small fraction of its initial level, and the remaining radioactivity is dominated by longer-lived isotopes that decay more slowly. Official guidance that authorizes movement at 24–72 hours typically reflects local conditions, wind patterns, and monitoring data — not a universal threshold. Follow the specific instructions for your area.

If you have a personal radiation detector, you can use it as a secondary data point — but do not override official guidance based on a personal reading. Shelter-in-Place vs Evacuation — Which Is Right?

This is one of the most consequential decisions in a nuclear emergency, and it depends entirely on circumstances that require official situational awareness to evaluate correctly. General principles from FEMA Ready.gov and DHS nuclear preparedness guidance:

  • Immediately after a detonation — shelter in place is almost always the right initial action. Getting into a building and away from fallout in the first 10–15 minutes dramatically reduces exposure. Attempting to drive through a fallout zone during peak intensity is far more dangerous than sheltering in an imperfect location.
  • Evacuation may be appropriate once official guidance is issued confirming routes are clear, fallout patterns are known, and a destination less affected by fallout is reachable without passing through high-exposure areas.
  • Never evacuate based solely on panic or social media. The decision requires official information about wind direction, detonation location, and current fallout distribution — information that emergency managers have and the public does not in real time.

Safety Tip

The physics is on your side — but only if you act immediately. The difference between being outside during peak fallout and being inside a concrete building during the same period can represent a dose reduction of 90% or more. You do not need a bunker. You need a plan, a supply kit, and the knowledge that getting inside immediately — even in an ordinary building — is one of the highest-impact survival decisions available to you in the first minutes after a detonation event. Decay at a Glance

Hour 1: Peak intensity — stay inside

Hour 7: ~10% of peak — stay inside

Hour 49: ~1% of peak — await official guidance

2 weeks: Fraction of original — follow official instructions to move
